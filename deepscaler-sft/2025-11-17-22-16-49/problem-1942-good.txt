### Step-by-Step Solution:

1. **Understand the Problem:**
    - We are given that \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\).
    - The projection of a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) onto another vector \(\mathbf{a}\) is given by:
      \[
      \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}} \mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{a}}{\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{a}} \mathbf{a}
      \]
    - For \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\), this means that \(\begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\) is a scalar multiple of \(\mathbf{a}\).

2. **Find Vector \(\mathbf{a}\):**
    - Let \(\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix}\).
    - Then, \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{a}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix}}{\begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix}} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\).
    - This implies that \(\frac{13a_2}{a_1^2 + a_2^2} \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\).
    - Therefore, we have:
      \[
      \frac{13a_2}{a_1^2 + a_2^2} a_1 = 6 \quad \text{(1)}
      \]
      \[
      \frac{13a_2}{a_1^2 + a_2^2} a_2 = 4 \quad \text{(2)}
      \]

3. **Solve for \(\mathbf{a}\):**
    - From equation (2), we get:
      \[
      \frac{13a_2^2}{a_1^2 + a_2^2} = 4 \implies 13a_2^2 = 4(a_1^2 + a_2^2) \implies 13a_2^2 = 4a_1^2 + 4a_2^2 \implies 9a_2^2 = 4a_1^2 \implies \frac{a_2^2}{a_1^2} = \frac{4}{9} \implies \frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{2}{3} \text{ or } \frac{a_2}{a_1} = -\frac{2}{3}
      \]
    - Substituting \(\frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{2}{3}\) into equation (1):
      \[
      \frac{13 \cdot \frac{2}{3} a_1}{a_1^2 + \left(\frac{2}{3} a_1\right)^2} a_1 = 6 \implies \frac{13 \cdot \frac{2}{3} a_1}{a_1^2 + \frac{4}{9} a_1^2} a_1 = 6 \implies \frac{13 \cdot \frac{2}{3} a_1}{\frac{13}{9} a_1^2} a_1 = 6 \implies \frac{13 \cdot 2 \cdot 9}{3 \cdot 13} = 6 \implies \frac{18}{3} = 6 \implies 6 = 6
      \]
      This confirms that \(\frac{a_2}{a_1} = \frac{2}{3}\) is a solution. So, we can write \(\mathbf{a} = k \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\) for some scalar \(k\). To normalize, we take \(\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\) (we can ignore the scalar because it will cancel out in the projection formula).

4. **Orthogonality Condition:**
    - Since \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{b}\) are orthogonal, \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = 0\).
    - Let \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{pmatrix}\), then the orthogonality condition gives:
      \[
      3 b_1 + 2 b_2 = 0 \implies b_2 = -\frac{3}{2} b_1
      \]
      We can choose \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}\) (normalized scaling does not affect the projection as long as the direction is maintained).

5. **Find \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix}\):**
    - The projection of \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix}\) onto \(\mathbf{b}\) is:
      \[
      \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}}{\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}
      \]
    - First, calculate the dot products:
      \[
      \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = 0 \cdot 2 + 13 \cdot (-3) = -39
      \]
      \[
      \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = 2^2 + (-3)^2 = 4 + 9 = 13
      \]
    - So, the projection becomes:
      \[
      \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf{b}} \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 13 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{-39}{13} \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = -3 \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -6 \\ 9 \end{pmatrix}
      \]
