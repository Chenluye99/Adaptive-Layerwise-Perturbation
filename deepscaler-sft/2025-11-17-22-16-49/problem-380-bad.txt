To solve this problem, we need to consider the properties of a right-angled triangle. In a right-angled triangle, the Pythagorean theorem holds, which states:

\[ a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are the lengths of the two legs, and \(c\) is the length of the hypotenuse.

We are given two side lengths of the triangle as 5 cm and 12 cm, and we know that one of the angles in the triangle is a right angle. This means that the two given sides can either be the two legs or one of them could be the hypotenuse.

Let's consider these two cases separately:

1. **Case 1: Both 5 cm and 12 cm are legs of the right-angled triangle.**

   In this case, the hypotenuse \(c\) can be calculated using the Pythagorean theorem:
   \[
   c = \sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{25 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13
   \]
   So, the third side (hypotenuse) is 13 cm.

2. **Case 2: One of the sides (either 5 cm or 12 cm) is the hypotenuse.**

   - **Subcase 2.1: 12 cm is the hypotenuse.**
     Then, the other two sides (say, 5 cm and the unknown side, let's call it \(x\)) would be the legs. We can use the Pythagorean theorem to find the unknown side:
     \[
     5^2 + x^2 = 12^2 \implies 25 + x^2 = 144 \implies x^2 = 119 \implies x = \sqrt{119}
     \]
     \[
     x \approx \sqrt{119} \approx 10.91
     \]
   - **Subcase 2.2: 5 cm is the hypotenuse.**
     This is not possible because the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle must be the longest side, and 5 cm is shorter than 12 cm. So, 5 cm cannot be the hypotenuse if 12 cm and 5 cm are the two given sides of the triangle.

Hence, the only viable cases are when 12 cm is the hypotenuse, and when both 5 cm and 12 cm are the legs of the triangle (in which case, 13 cm is the hypotenuse).

So, the possible lengths for the third side are 13 cm (if 5 and 12 are the legs) and \(\sqrt{119} \approx 10.91\) cm (if 12 is the hypotenuse).

The shortest possible length of the third side is therefore \(\sqrt{119} \approx 10.91\) cm.
