First, let's understand what it means for a number to have exactly five distinct positive factors. If the prime factorization of a number \( n \) is given by \( n = p_1^{e_1} \cdot p_2^{e_2} \cdot \ldots \cdot p_k^{e_k} \), then the total number of factors of \( n \) is given by \((e_1 + 1) \cdot (e_2 + 1) \cdot \ldots \cdot (e_k + 1)\).

For the number of factors to be exactly 5, the expression \((e_1 + 1) \cdot (e_2 + 1) \cdot \ldots \cdot (e_k + 1) = 5\) must hold true. The number 5 is a prime number, so its only factorizations are \( 5 = 5 \cdot 1 \) and \( 5 = 1 \cdot 5 \).

This implies that the number must be of the form:
1. \( p_1^4 \) (because \( 4 + 1 = 5 \)), or
2. \( p_1 \cdot p_2^4 \) (because it is not possible for a number to have more than one distinct prime factor such that the product of their incremented exponents equals 5, since 5 itself is a product of 5 and 1, which corresponds to just one prime factor raised to the 4th power).

Hence, the smallest number of the form \( p_1^4 \) is when \( p_1 = 2 \), giving \( 2^4 = 16 \). The smallest number of the form \( p_1 \cdot p_2^4 \) would be when \( p_1 = 2 \) and \( p_2 = 3 \), giving \( 2 \cdot 3^4 = 2 \cdot 81 = 162 \). Clearly, 16 is smaller than 162, so we start by checking 16 and other numbers of the form \( p_1^4 \) in ascending order, ensuring we check for the smallest one with exactly 5 factors.

We can use Python to check this algorithmically. We'll write a Python function to determine the number of factors of each positive integer and find the smallest integer that has exactly 5 factors.
```python
def count_factors(n):
    count = 0
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        if n % i == 0:
            count += 1
    return count

# Now, we need to find the smallest positive integer that has exactly 5 factors
num = 1
while True:
    if count_factors(num) == 5:
        break
    num += 1
```
