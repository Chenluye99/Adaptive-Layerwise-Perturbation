To solve the problem, let's model the probability \( p \) that the tetrahedral die and all its copies will eventually disappear. 

We denote:
- \( p_0 \) as the probability that the die and all its copies will eventually disappear starting from a single die that landed on 0.
- \( p_1 \) as the probability that the die and all its copies will eventually disappear starting from a single die that landed on 1.
- \( p_2 \) as the probability that the die and all its copies will eventually disappear starting from a single die that landed on 2.
- \( p_3 \) as the probability that the die and all its copies will eventually disappear starting from a single die that landed on 3.

First, let's establish the following observations based on the rules of the problem:

- If the die lands on 0, it disappears, so \( p_0 = 1 \).
- If the die lands on 1, nothing happens, so the probability that the die and all its copies will eventually disappear is just \( p_1 \), so \( p_1 = p_1 \).
- If the die lands on 2, two more copies of the die appear, so we get a total of three dice, and the probability that these three dice will all eventually disappear is \( p_2 \cdot p_2 \cdot p_2 = p_2^3 \), so \( p_2 = p_2^3 \).
- If the die lands on 3, three more copies of the die appear, so we get a total of four dice, and the probability that these four dice will all eventually disappear is \( p_3 \cdot p_3 \cdot p_3 \cdot p_3 = p_3^4 \), so \( p_3 = p_3^4 \).

Now, we need to express \( p_1, p_2, \) and \( p_3 \) in terms of a single entity. Consider that initially, the die can land on any of the four faces with equal probability, so the overall probability \( p \) of all the dice eventually disappearing satisfies:
\[ p = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p_1 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p_2^3 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p_3^4. \]

Next, we need to solve the equations \( p_2 = p_2^3 \) and \( p_3 = p_3^4 \):

1. For \( p_2 = p_2^3 \):
\[ p_2^3 - p_2 = 0 \implies p_2 (p_2^2 - 1) = 0 \implies p_2 (p_2 - 1)(p_2 + 1) = 0. \]
This gives us the solutions:
\[ p_2 = 0, \, p_2 = 1, \, p_2 = -1. \]
Since a probability must be in the range \([0, 1]\), we have:
\[ p_2 = 0 \text{ or } p_2 = 1. \]

2. For \( p_3 = p_3^4 \):
\[ p_3^4 - p_3 = 0 \implies p_3 (p_3^3 - 1) = 0 \implies p_3 (p_3 - 1)(p_3^2 + p_3 + 1) = 0. \]
This gives us the solutions:
\[ p_3 = 0, \, p_3 = 1, \, p_3 = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{3}i}{2}, \, p_3 = \frac{-1 - \sqrt{3}i}{2}. \]
Since a probability must be a real number in the range \([0, 1]\), we have:
\[ p_3 = 0 \text{ or } p_3 = 1. \]

We need to consider realistic scenarios. If \( p_2 = 0 \) or \( p_3 = 0 \), then it would be very unlikely for the system to eventually disappear because it would imply that the probability of eventual disappearance vanishes if even a single copy remains. So, it's more reasonable to assume \( p_2 = 1 \) and \( p_3 = 1 \) because if even a single copy cannot disappear, it significantly decreases the chances of eventual disappearance of the whole system. 

If we set \( p_2 = 1 \) and \( p_3 = 1 \), then the equations simplify to:
\[ p = \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p_1 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1^3 + \frac{1}{4} \cdot 1^4 = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p_1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p_1. \]

To find out \( p_1 \), we consider that if the die lands on 1, the probability that the die and all its copies will eventually disappear starting from this die is just \( p \) because it does not change how future dice rolls eventually lead to all dice disappearing. Thus, we can substitute it back and assume:
\[ p_1 = p. \]
Substitute this back into our earlier equation, we get:
\[ p = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \cdot p. \]

Now, solve for \( p \):
\[ p - \frac{1}{4} \cdot p = \frac{1}{2}, \]
\[ \frac{3}{4} \cdot p = \frac{1}{2}, \]
\[ p = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{4}{3} = \frac{2}{3}. \]

So, we have \( p = \frac{2}{3} \). Now, we need to compute \(\left\lfloor \frac{10}{p} \right\rfloor \):
\[ \frac{10}{p} = \frac{10}{\frac{2}{3}} = 10 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 15. \]
Thus,
\[ \left\lfloor \frac{10}{p} \right\rfloor = 15. \]
