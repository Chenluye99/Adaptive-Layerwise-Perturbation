First, let's start by finding the area of triangle \( \triangle CDA \). Given that \( CD = DA = 10 \) and \(\angle CDA = 60^\circ \), we can use the formula for the area of a triangle when two sides and the included angle are known:

\[
\text{Area}_{\triangle CDA} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot CD \cdot DA \cdot \sin(\angle CDA)
\]

Substituting the given values:

\[
\text{Area}_{\triangle CDA} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \sin(60^\circ) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 25 \sqrt{3}
\]

Next, we need to find the length of \( CA \). We can use the Law of Cosines for this:

\[
CA^2 = CD^2 + DA^2 - 2 \cdot CD \cdot DA \cdot \cos(\angle CDA)
\]

Substituting the given values:

\[
CA^2 = 10^2 + 10^2 - 2 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \cos(60^\circ) = 100 + 100 - 2 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 100 + 100 - 100 = 100
\]

Thus,

\[
CA = \sqrt{100} = 10
\]

Now, we need to find the area of triangle \( \triangle ABC \). We know the lengths of \( AB = 8 \), \( BC = 4 \), and \( CA = 10 \). We can use Heron's formula to find its area. First, we calculate the semi-perimeter \( s \) of triangle \( \triangle ABC \):

\[
s = \frac{AB + BC + CA}{2} = \frac{8 + 4 + 10}{2} = 11
\]

Then, we use Heron's formula to find the area:

\[
\text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} = \sqrt{s(s - AB)(s - BC)(s - CA)} = \sqrt{11(11 - 8)(11 - 4)(11 - 10)} = \sqrt{11 \cdot 3 \cdot 7 \cdot 1} = \sqrt{231}
\]

So, the total area of quadrilateral \( ABCD \) is the sum of the areas of \( \triangle ABC \) and \( \triangle CDA \):

\[
\text{Area}_{ABCD} = \text{Area}_{\triangle ABC} + \text{Area}_{\triangle CDA} = \sqrt{231} + 25 \sqrt{3}
\]

Here, \(\sqrt{231}\) is already in its simplest radical form because 231 factors into \(3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11\), and none of these factors (other than 1) are perfect squares.

\[
\sqrt{231} = \sqrt{a} \quad \text{and} \quad 25 \sqrt{3} = b \sqrt{c}
