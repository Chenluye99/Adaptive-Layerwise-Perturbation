To solve this problem, let's define the variables and constraints:

1. Jenny's grandmother has 24 cats in total.
2. Seventeen of the cats do not catch mice.
3. Ten of the cats have black fur.

We want to find the smallest possible number of cats that do not catch mice and have black fur.

Let's denote:
- \( A \) as the set of cats that do not catch mice.
- \( B \) as the set of cats that have black fur.

We know:
- \( |A| = 17 \)
- \( |B| = 10 \)

We want to find the smallest possible value of \( |A \cap B| \) (the number of cats that do not catch mice and have black fur).

To approach this, we can use the principle of inclusion and exclusion for sets. The number of cats that either do not catch mice or have black fur (or both) is limited by the total number of cats.

First, let's determine how many cats either do not catch mice or have black fur (or both):

\[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| - |A \cap B| \]

Since the total number of cats is 24, we have:

\[ |A \cup B| \leq 24 \]

Substituting the values for \( |A| \) and \( |B| \):

\[ 17 + 10 - |A \cap B| \leq 24 \]

This simplifies to:

\[ 27 - |A \cap B| \leq 24 \]

Rearranging gives:

\[ |A \cap B| \geq 3 \]

This means the smallest possible number of cats that do not catch mice and have black fur is 3. To ensure this is possible, we can construct a scenario where it is true:

- We have 17 cats that do not catch mice.
- We have 10 cats that have black fur.
- If only 3 of the cats that have black fur also do not catch mice, then the remaining 7 cats that have black fur must be among the cats that catch mice.

This scenario is consistent with the constraints, so the smallest possible number of cats that do not catch mice and have black fur is 3.

# Number of cats that do not catch mice

# Total number of cats

min_overlap = None

    union = cats_not_catching_mice + cats_black_fur - overlap
    if union <= total_cats:
