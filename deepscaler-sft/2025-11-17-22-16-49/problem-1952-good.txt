First, let's set up the problem. When a right triangle is rotated about one of its legs, it forms a cone. The formula for the volume \(V\) of a cone is given by:

\[ V = \frac{1}{3} \pi r^2 h \]

where \( r \) is the radius of the base of the cone, and \( h \) is the height of the cone.

1. **Case 1: Rotate around AB (which has length 3 cm):**
    - When rotating around \( AB \), the radius \( r \) of the base of the cone will be the length of \( CB = 4 \) cm, and the height \( h \) of the cone will be the length of \( AB = 3 \) cm.
    - Substituting these into the volume formula, we get:
      \[
      V_1 = \frac{1}{3} \pi (4)^2 (3) = \frac{1}{3} \pi \cdot 16 \cdot 3 = 16 \pi
      \]

2. **Case 2: Rotate around CB (which has length 4 cm):**
    - When rotating around \( CB \), the radius \( r \) of the base of the cone will be the length of \( AB = 3 \) cm, and the height \( h \) of the cone will be the length of \( CB = 4 \) cm.
    - Substituting these into the volume formula, we get:
      \[
      V_2 = \frac{1}{3} \pi (3)^2 (4) = \frac{1}{3} \pi \cdot 9 \cdot 4 = 12 \pi
      \]

To determine the greatest possible volume, we compare the volumes obtained from both cases:

\[ V_1 = 16 \pi \]
\[ V_2 = 12 \pi \]

Since \(\pi\) is a common factor, it's clear that \(16 \pi\) is greater than \(12 \pi\). So, the greatest possible volume is \(16 \pi\).

To get the answer in cubic centimeters and to the nearest whole number, we can calculate the numerical value of \(16 \pi\) and round it to the nearest whole number.
