To solve this problem, we need to determine the smallest possible number of 2-by-3 rectangles that can be used to exactly cover a square region without any overhang or gaps.

### Step-by-Step Reasoning:

1. **Determine the Area Requirements:**
    - The area of a 2-by-3 rectangle is \(2 \times 3 = 6\).
    - To cover a square exactly, the area of the square must be a multiple of 6 (since it must be perfectly divisible by 6 to be covered exactly by the 2-by-3 rectangles without any gaps or overhangs).

2. **Find the Smallest Possible Square:**
    - Let the side length of the square be \( n \). Then, the area of the square is \( n^2 \).
    - We need \( n^2 \) to be a multiple of 6. This means that \( n^2 \) must be divisible by both 2 and 3, so \( n \) itself must be divisible by both 2 and 3 (i.e., \( n \) must be divisible by 6, because the least common multiple of 2 and 3 is 6).

3. **Check Starting Values:**
    - The smallest value of \( n \) that satisfies this condition is 6, because \( 6 = \text{lcm}(2, 3) \).
    - For \( n = 6 \), the area of the square is \( 6^2 = 36 \). Now, we need to check if 36 can be perfectly covered by 2-by-3 rectangles. The number of such rectangles required would be \(\frac{36}{6} = 6 \).
