To solve this problem, let's start by understanding the geometric configuration of the two circles.

1. **Problem Understanding:**
    - Each circle has a radius of 10 cm.
    - Each circle passes through the center of the other circle. This means the distance between the centers of the two circles is equal to the radius of the circles, which is 10 cm.
    - We need to find the length of the common chord of the two circles.

2. **Geometric Insight:**
    - When the circles overlap in such a way that each circle passes through the center of the other, the common chord will be a perpendicular bisector of the line segment joining the two centers of the circles. In this case, the centers of the circles are 10 cm apart and each circle has a radius of 10 cm, so the chord we're looking for is actually the base of an equilateral triangle formed by the centers of the circles and one of the points of intersection of the circles.
    - The length of the common chord can be found by noting that the perpendicular dropped from the center of a circle to the chord bisects the chord and forms two right triangles with the radius and half the chord length as legs, and the distance between the centers of the circles as the base of these right triangles.

3. **Formalize and Calculate:**
    - Draw a line connecting the centers of the two circles, forming a line segment of length 10 cm.
    - Draw the common chord, which we'll call \( AB \), and drop perpendiculars from the centers of the circles to the chord, meeting the chord at points that bisect it, let's call them \( M \) and \( M' \), such that \( M \) and \( M' \) are the midpoints of \( AB \).
    - Since the circles have the same radius and the line connecting their centers is 10 cm (which is equal to the radius), forming two right triangles with legs of lengths 5 cm each (half of 10 cm), and the hypotenuse of 10 cm each (the radii of the circles).

    Using the properties of these right triangles, the length of half of the chord \( AB \) can be determined by using the Pythagorean theorem. Specifically, if we consider a right triangle formed by a radius, half the chord, and the distance between the centers, we get:
    \[
    \left(\frac{AB}{2}\right)^2 + 5^2 = 10^2
    \]
    \[
    \left(\frac{AB}{2}\right)^2 + 25 = 100
    \]
    \[
    \left(\frac{AB}{2}\right)^2 = 75
    \]
    \[
    \frac{AB}{2} = \sqrt{75} = 5 \sqrt{3}
    \]
    \[
    AB = 2 \cdot 5 \sqrt{3} = 10 \sqrt{3}
    \]

Let's confirm this by coding it out as well.
```python
import sympy as sp

# Define the variables
x, y = sp.symbols('x y')

# Equations of the circles
circle1 = x**2 + y**2 - 10**2
circle2 = (x - 10)**2 + y**2 - 10**2

# Find the intersection points of the circles
intersection_points = sp.solve([circle1, circle2], (x, y))

print(intersection_points)
```
Code execution result: [(5, -5*sqrt(3)), (5, 5*sqrt(3))]

From the code, we get the intersection points as \((5, -5 \sqrt{3})\) and \((5, 5 \sqrt{3})\). The common chord is the line segment connecting these points, and its length is the distance between \((5, -5 \sqrt{3})\) and \((5, 5 \sqrt{3})\).

4. **Calculate the Length of the Chord:**
    - The length of the chord is calculated as the difference in the y-coordinates, since the x-coordinates are the same for both points:
    \[
    \text{Length of chord} = 5 \sqrt{3} - (-5 \sqrt{3}) = 10 \sqrt{3}
    \]

So, the length of the common chord is \(\boxed{10 \sqrt{3}}\). This matches our previous reasoning.
